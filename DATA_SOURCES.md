# Data Sources — Free & Legitimate

Split by the two models, because their situations are completely different:

- **Drone imagery (CNN)** — *tractable*. Good free data exists. This is where your
  effort pays off.
- **Tabular sensor data** — *genuinely hard*. Real open-pit slope radar and
  extensometer data is proprietary industrial data and essentially not public. Be
  honest about this rather than pretending otherwise.

Always check each dataset's licence before using it, and cite it in your README.

---

## Part 1 — Drone / crack imagery (the CNN)

### Strategy: pretrain broad, fine-tune narrow

You said you have some drone pictures. That's the valuable part, but almost
certainly not enough to train a CNN from scratch. The standard approach:

1. **Pretrain** on a large public crack dataset (tens of thousands of images).
2. **Fine-tune** on your own labelled pit-wall images (a few hundred is workable).
3. **Evaluate** on a held-out set of *your* images — that is the number you quote.

Better still: skip the custom `PitWallCNN` entirely and fine-tune a pretrained
ResNet18 or EfficientNet-B0 backbone. A small custom CNN trained on a few thousand
images will lose to a fine-tuned ImageNet backbone almost every time, and "I used
transfer learning because my dataset was too small to train from scratch" is a
better interview answer than a bespoke architecture that underperforms.

### Datasets

**SDNET2018** — the best starting point.
56,000+ annotated images of cracked and non-cracked concrete (bridge decks, walls,
pavements), 256×256, cracks from 0.06 mm to 25 mm. Deliberately includes shadows,
surface roughness, edges and debris — the same confounders your pit-wall images
will have. Free from Utah State University: `https://doi.org/10.15142/T3TD19`
(Dorafshan, Thomas & Maguire, 2018).

Concrete isn't rock, and you should say so — but crack *morphology* transfers
well, and this is far better than starting from random weights.

**Roboflow Universe** — `https://universe.roboflow.com`, search "rock crack",
"crack detection", "mining". Smaller datasets (hundreds to a few thousand images),
mostly CC-licensed, and — usefully — Roboflow will also host and augment *your*
drone images and export in YOLO/COCO/PyTorch formats. Free tier is enough here.

**Kaggle** — search "surface crack detection" (the Özgenel concrete crack dataset,
~40k 227×227 images, clean binary labels) and "Open Pit Mine Object Detection
Dataset". Your `.gitignore` already references
`data/Open-Pit-Mine-Object-Detection-Dataset/`, so you had this one at some point
— worth locating again since it's the closest to your actual domain.

**GCCMA-UAV** — 6,280 UAV images of ground cracks in coal mining areas at 3 cm
resolution, 256×256. The closest published match to your use case. Introduced in
the DRA-UNet paper (Remote Sensing, 2024); availability is via the authors, so
check the paper's data-availability statement.

**CrackForest / DeepCrack / Crack500** — pavement crack *segmentation* datasets
with pixel masks. Useful if you later want crack extent and width rather than
just a binary classification. CrackForest is small (118 images) but well-labelled.

### Labelling your own images

Use **Label Studio** (free, open source) or Roboflow's annotator. For a binary
classifier you only need folder-level labels — `cracked/` and `intact/` — which
is quick. Guidance that matters:

- **Aim for a few hundred per class minimum.** Below ~100 per class the test set
  is too small for the metrics to mean anything.
- **Split by flight or location, not randomly.** Two crops from the same photo in
  train and test is the image-domain version of the temporal-leak problem that
  invalidated your original accuracy claim.
- **Label ambiguous cases consistently**, and keep a written rule for what counts
  as a crack. Inconsistent labels put a ceiling on achievable accuracy.
- **Keep the confounders in.** Shadows, wet rock, machinery tracks, vegetation.
  Removing them inflates your score and produces a model that fails in the field.

## Part 2 — Tabular sensor data (the risk model)

### The honest situation

There is no public dataset of open-pit slope radar, extensometer, piezometer and
geophone readings with labelled failure outcomes. This data is generated by
commercial monitoring systems (GroundProbe, IDS GeoRadar, Reutech), owned by mine
operators, and not released — partly commercial sensitivity, partly liability.

Saying this plainly in your README is a strength. "The data doesn't exist publicly,
so here is what I did instead and here are the limitations" reads as engineering
maturity. Claiming otherwise is a claim someone will check.

### Option A — Real landslide events joined to real weather (recommended)

This is the strongest available path, and it's a genuinely good project on its own.

**NASA COOLR / Global Landslide Catalog** — `https://landslides.nasa.gov/viewer`
11,000+ rainfall-triggered landslide events worldwide since 2007, with date,
latitude/longitude, trigger type, and size. Free, downloadable, with an ArcGIS
REST API. Cite Kirschbaum et al. (2015, 2010) and Juang et al. (2019).

**Join it with Open-Meteo's historical weather API** — which is free, needs no key,
and *you already use it in this project*. For each landslide event, pull the
preceding 7–30 days of hourly precipitation at those coordinates. Sample negative
examples from the same regions on non-event dates.

You now have a **real** dataset: antecedent rainfall → landslide occurrence. Real
labels, real weather, real locations. It won't have pore pressure or displacement,
so it can't replace the full feature set — but it makes the rainfall→instability
half of your model empirically grounded rather than invented, and it gives you a
defensible answer to "where did your data come from?"

Watch for two traps: the catalog is media-report-derived and so is biased toward
populated areas and fatal events, and constructing negatives requires care —
"nothing was reported" is not the same as "nothing happened."

**Landslide4Sense** — satellite multispectral imagery with landslide segmentation
masks from the 2022 competition, if you want to go the remote-sensing route.

**USGS and GSI (India) landslide inventories** — regional, sometimes with more
geotechnical detail than COOLR.

### Option B — Keep synthetic, but be rigorous

This is what `ml/train_model.py` now does, and it's legitimate *provided* you label
it clearly. Key requirements, all already implemented:

- overlapping class distributions (no trivially separable feature)
- persistent regime state, not independent per-hour sampling
- realistic class imbalance
- confounders (machinery noise) that punish naive feature use
- silent failures, so recall has a realistic ceiling
- temporal splits
- every metric labelled "synthetic benchmark"

The pitfall to watch: if your synthetic benchmark returns a near-perfect score, the
benchmark is broken. I hit this twice while writing the generator — first with
well-separated velocity distributions, then with a seismic feature that only
Critical had. Both produced a fake 1.000. That debugging story is worth telling.

### Option C — Physical modelling literature

Slope stability has decades of published work: limit-equilibrium methods
(Bishop, Morgenstern-Price), the inverse-velocity method (Fukuzono, 1985) for
predicting failure time from accelerating creep, and rainfall-threshold curves
(Guzzetti et al.). Grounding your synthetic generator's parameters in published
ranges — and citing them — is far more defensible than picking numbers that look
plausible. The inverse-velocity method in particular is directly relevant to your
`velocity_mm_h` and `acceleration_mm_h2` features and is worth reading before an
interview, since it's the domain-standard technique your project is adjacent to.

## Suggested order of work

1. **Fix the untrained-CNN honesty issue first** (see `MODEL_CARD.md`). Either
   train it or label it accurately. This matters more than adding data.
2. **Label your existing drone images.** Even 200 per class beats zero.
3. **Pretrain on SDNET2018, fine-tune on yours.** Report metrics on your held-out
   images, split by flight.
4. **Build the COOLR + Open-Meteo dataset.** A weekend of work, and it converts
   "I generated synthetic data" into "I built a real dataset by joining two public
   sources" — a much better story.
5. **Keep the synthetic pipeline** for the full 13-feature model, clearly labelled.
